# Larder Sales Kit

A working playbook for pitching Larder to a real venue owner, in person, by phone, or over coffee. This is v1, the hand delivered service stage. There is no paid ad funnel and no cold outreach sequence here, because there shouldn't be one yet. Read this once, then go have the conversation.

This document is Larder's own material, so it follows the same voice rules as the product: plain verbs, sentence case, no filler, active voice, confident and direct, never condescending to a busy owner who has heard software pitches before and been burned by some of them.

There is no paying customer yet. This kit does not contain a case study, a testimonial, or a "venues like yours" claim, because none of that exists yet and inventing it would break trust with the exact person we are trying to earn trust from. Where a sales conversation would normally lean on past results, lean on the live demo, the mechanism itself, and the founder's own credibility instead.

---

## 1. Qualifying questions

Ask these before you pitch anything. The goal is not to qualify the venue in or out, almost any independent venue with 10 to 30 staff is a fit, the goal is to find out which pain to lead with so the pitch adapts to what you hear rather than running one fixed script.

1. **How many staff do you currently have, and how often do you hire?** This tells you which pricing tier applies and how often the onboarding pain actually recurs.
2. **Walk me through what happens on a new hire's first shift right now.** Listen for: a folder, a PDF nobody reads, a senior staff member pulled off the floor to explain everything verbally, or genuinely nothing written down at all.
3. **How do you currently track RSA, food handling, first aid, and Working with Children checks, and when they expire?** Listen for: a spreadsheet, a wall calendar, someone's memory, or an honest "we don't, really."
4. **When a staff member has a question mid shift and a manager isn't around, what do they do?** Listen for how often the same senior person gets pulled away from real work to answer the same repeated questions.
5. **What's the most frustrating part of running training and onboarding here today?** Ask this open ended and let them talk. The answer tells you which section of the pitch to lead with, don't lead with pricing or mechanism before you've heard this.

Do not skip these to get to the pitch faster. A pitch that answers a pain the owner didn't mention lands as generic software talk. A pitch that answers the exact thing they just complained about lands as "this person actually listened."

## 2. The 30 second version

Say this out loud, adjust the bracketed pain to whatever came out of the qualifying questions:

> "Larder turns your own SOPs, your own photos, and how your own staff actually work into training modules built specifically for your venue, not generic hospitality content. New hires get an always on chatbot that answers questions from your procedures only, so [whoever currently gets interrupted] isn't stuck re explaining the same thing every week. You get a dashboard that tracks who's actually finished training and when everyone's RSA, food safety, and other certificates expire, with reminders before they lapse, not after an inspector asks. Nothing goes live to your staff until you've reviewed and approved it yourself. And it's month to month, no lock in contract, so there's no risk in trying it."

That is the whole pitch in one breath. Everything below is what to say when they ask a follow up question, because they will.

## 4. Objection handling

Real objections a skeptical small business owner will actually raise. Answer them honestly, grounded in the real product and the real pricing. Don't deflect, and don't oversell.

| Objection | Honest answer |
|---|---|
| "This is too expensive for a small venue." | The setup fee scales with venue size: $799 for one to five staff, $1,299 for six to fifteen, $1,999 for sixteen to twenty five, and a custom quote for anything larger. The monthly retainer is a flat $100 regardless of tier, which includes tracking, reminders, and five content edits a month. For a first venue, there's a discretionary founder's rate of $750 to make trying it lower risk. Compare that to the cost of one missed inspection or one bad shift caused by a new hire who was never properly shown what to do. |
| "We already train staff fine informally." | That might be true today, while the same two or three senior people are around to answer everything. The question is what happens on the day one of them is off, sick, or leaves. Larder doesn't replace how the venue trains, it captures how the venue already does things and makes sure that knowledge doesn't live only in one person's head. |
| "AI will just make things up." | That's a fair concern and it's the first thing to address directly. Ask Larder only answers from that specific venue's own approved content, retrieval is filtered at the database level before anything reaches the chatbot, it can't pull from another venue's information and it can't invent an answer outside what's been approved. For anything involving keys, safes, alarms, or logins, it's built to always say to ask a supervisor rather than guess. That's a locked rule, not a suggestion to the AI. |
| "What happens to our data if we cancel?" | Nothing gets held hostage. It's month to month with thirty days' written notice, and on cancellation the venue's own content and completion records export in a standard format. Chatbot access ends when the notice period closes. There's no lock in by design, because getting trapped in software is the single biggest reason small business owners avoid signing up for it in the first place. |
| "This feels like a big process change for a busy kitchen." | The build work happens on the venue's side without disrupting a shift, it's built from SOPs, photos, and short interviews, not a long implementation project. And nothing goes live until the owner has reviewed and approved every module, so there's no surprise rollout to staff. It's designed to fit around a busy kitchen, not add a project on top of it. |
| "How do I know this actually works before I commit?" | There's a real, live demo venue built on the actual product, not a mockup or a slide deck. It can be shown right now, or a link and a login can be sent together so it can be explored hands on afterward, including trying the chatbot directly. The link alone won't get anyone in; a login always goes with it. That's the strongest proof available at this stage, because it's real and interactive rather than a claim. |

## 5. How to present the pricing

Pricing should feel like a straightforward, honest number, not a reveal at the end of a script. Bring it up naturally once the owner understands the mechanism, don't hide it and don't lead with it either.

**What to say in the first conversation:**
- The setup fee tier that matches their staff count, stated plainly as a number, not "starting from."
- The $100 a month retainer and what it actually includes: the dashboard, completion tracking, certificate expiry tracking, the weekly digest, and five free content edits a month.
- That it's month to month with thirty days' notice, no contract lock in.
- If they're a strong candidate for a first client, the founder's rate, framed honestly as "I'm offering a reduced rate to get the first real venue on this and prove it out," not as a fake discount from an inflated price.

**What can wait for a follow up conversation:**
- The $15 per additional edit rate beyond the five included. Mention it exists if asked, don't lead with it.
- The complimentary formal comprehension quiz add on, worth mentioning once they're already interested, not as a headline feature.
- SOP generation for procedures the venue has never written down at all. This is a separate itemised service quoted after a walkthrough of what actually needs documenting, don't quote a number before that walkthrough happens.
- The Large tier custom quote for venues over twenty five staff, since it depends on specifics worth a real conversation.

Never present pricing apologetically. State it, then stop talking. Let the owner react before filling the silence.

## 7. FAQ

**Is this a full replacement for in person training?**
No. It's built to support in person training, not replace the judgment of a real supervisor. The fallback rule specifically hands off anything requiring physical or system access to a real person.

**Does the chatbot know things about other venues?**
No. Retrieval is filtered to that specific venue before anything reaches the chatbot. It can only see and answer from that one venue's own approved content.

**Who writes the training content?**
It's built from the venue's own SOPs, photos, and short interviews with the owner or senior staff, not bought off the shelf generic hospitality material. The owner approves every module before it goes live to staff.

**Does this work for a venue that has never written any procedures down?**
Yes, that's actually a common starting point. The build process itself creates that documentation for the first time, and the SOP document generator turns the approved training content into a proper printable procedures document as a side benefit.

**Is RSA or WWCC content the same everywhere in Australia?**
No, and this matters. RSA and Working with Children Check requirements are state and territory specific, WWCC is even called something different in some states. Any compliance content is confirmed against that venue's actual state, never assumed from another state's rules.

**What's the e signature actually for?**
It's a staff member typing their name to confirm they've completed and understood a module, with a timestamp and device recorded, an in house comprehension record, not a legal contract signature.

**How long does setup actually take?**
This depends on the venue and hasn't been standardised yet since there's no completed first client to benchmark against. Be honest about this if asked rather than quoting a number that hasn't been proven.

## 8. Keeping this kit current

This is a living sales document, not a one time deliverable. Update it on the following triggers, not on a fixed schedule.

**Once there is a real first client:**
- Add one genuine, specific case study once a real venue has actually gone live and used the product for at least a few weeks, not before. Use the venue's real words if they'll allow a quote, attributed and permissioned, never paraphrased into something they didn't say.
- Replace the "no case study yet" honesty framing in the objection handling and FAQ sections with the real before and after, for example an actual reduction in how often a manager gets interrupted, or an actual certificate that got renewed on time because of a reminder that would previously have been missed. Only put a number in this document if it is a real number from a real venue, never a projected or estimated one.
- Keep the founder's rate language until there's a defensible reason to phase it out, it may still be the right offer for venue two or three while Larder is still proving the service model, not just venue one.

**When re running this exercise for a new pricing tier, a new venue type, or a new region:**
- Re run the qualifying questions section first with someone who actually represents that new segment, don't assume a pub's pain points transfer directly to a cafe's or a regional venue's. The pain point mapping in section 3 should be re validated, not copy pasted, a cafe's compliance pain may be different from a nightclub's.
- Re check every compliance claim against that region's actual regulator, especially RSA and WWCC naming and rules, before this kit or any pitch goes out for a new state or territory. Confirm against the Sources & References doc in Notion, don't assume Victoria's rules generalise.
- Re verify every pricing number against whatever is current in the Notion Decision Log before reusing this document, pricing in this kit is a snapshot from the time it was written, not a live source of truth.
- Keep the same structure, qualifying questions, thirty second version, pain mapping, objections, pricing presentation, sample script, FAQ, that structure is what makes this usable in a real conversation, don't let a future revision turn it back into a marketing brochure.
